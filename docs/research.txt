So can op amps also be used in the power supply circuit to somehow smooth a DC power source? shunt regulators like TL431 can help?

Yes, op-amps and adjustable precision shunt regulators like the TL431 are used in power supply designs to create smooth, precise, ultra-low-noise DC power rails.

However, because op-amps and shunt regulators are signal-level devices with very low current limits (typically 
<100 mA), they cannot power a high-current load directly. Instead, they serve as the precision control intelligence that drives a high-current transistor (pass element).

1. How Op-Amps Smooth and Regulate Power Supplies

what happens during a switch event? maybe using relays?

During a mechanical relay switch event—such as swapping battery banks or toggling a power rail—the circuit undergoes a rapid sequence of electrical, magnetic, and mechanical state changes over approximately 10 to 50 milliseconds.

Mechanical & Electrical Timeline
Coil Energization (
0 ms−5 ms)

When the control signal applies voltage to the relay coil, current begins to build through the coil windings. This creates a expanding magnetic field inside the iron core, which overcomes the physical tension of the internal return spring.

Contact Disconnection / Arcing Phase (
5 ms−12 ms)

As the movable armature pulls away from the initial stationary contact, the physical contact point snaps open.

Contact Arc: If current is actively flowing, the high energy density at the tiny opening contact point ionizes the air, causing a brief electrical arc.

Transient Voltage Spike: Switching inductive loads (like transformers or boost converter inductors) causes an instantaneous voltage spike (
V=L 
dt
di

Flight Time / Open Gap (
12 ms−20 ms)

In a standard Break-Before-Make (BBM) relay, there is a physical gap where the armature is moving between the two contacts. During this brief window, neither contact is connected, creating a temporary open circuit.

Contact Make & Contact Bounce (
20 ms−35 ms)

The armature impacts the second contact. Because the metal contacts have elasticity, they physically bounce off each other 2 to 5 times in rapid succession before settling. This "contact bounce" creates ultra-fast micro-interruptions in power.

Impact on an Audio Power Supply
When swapping batteries feeding a power amplifier or boost converter, a naive relay switch causes two distinct issues:

Rail Voltage Collapse: During the 
10 ms−20 ms "flight time" and contact bounce window, input power vanishes. The power supply rails drop to zero unless backed by heavy filter capacitors, causing an audible "thump" or trigger of the amp's protection circuit.

Contact Degradation: The arcing caused by switching under load gradually pits and oxidizes the contact material (typically silver-tin-oxide or silver-nickel), increasing contact resistance over time.

Mitigation Techniques for Clean Switching
Buffer Capacitor Hold-Up: Place a large electrolytic capacitor bank (
10,000μF+) downstream of the relay contacts. These capacitors discharge during the 
20 ms switching window to keep the DC rail voltage steady.

Solid-State Relays (SSRs / MOSFET Switches): Replacing mechanical relays with back-to-back N-channel Power MOSFETs eliminates mechanical travel and contact bounce entirely, completing the switch in less than 
1μs.

Flyback / Snubber Diodes: Always place a reverse-biased diode (e.g., 1N4007) across the relay coil to safely bleed off the coil's inductive high-voltage collapse spike when de-energized.

Where It Will Cause Issues: High-impedance audio preamps, sample-and-hold circuits, fast peak detectors, or wideband AC amplification where high slew rate and ultra-low input bias current are required.

So if I built all three audio amps, could I "see" the difference on a scope, when amplifying a function generator sine wave?

Yes, you can clearly see the differences on an oscilloscope, but where and how you see them depends on whether you look in time-domain (standard trace) or frequency-domain (FFT spectrum analyzer mode).

When driving a real load under high volume, each transistor pair reveals its physical limitations on the screen.

1. Standard Time-Domain Scope (Sine Wave Trace)
At low volume, all three pairs produce a recognizable sine wave. But as you push the function generator signal higher while driving an 
8Ω load on 
±6 V rails, clear visual differences emerge:

2N2222 / 2N2907 (Severe Peak Flattening & Asymmetry):
As current demand exceeds 
≈100 mA, current gain drops sharply. Instead of a smooth round peak, the top and bottom of the sine wave begin to flatten out well before reaching the supply rails (dynamic compression). Because NPN and PNP 2N-series transistors are loosely matched, one side flattens earlier than the other, creating visual wave asymmetry.

BC337 / BC327 (Clean Peaks, Sharp Hard Clipping):
The sine wave remains smooth and round right up until the amplifier hits maximum output. When it runs out of voltage headroom, it snaps instantly into sharp, flat-topped horizontal lines (hard clipping).

BD139 / BD140 (Tallest Peak Swing & Smooth Symmetry):
Because of their very low collector-emitter saturation voltage (
V 
CE(sat)
​
 ), the BD139/BD140 sine wave stretches visually taller on the grid than the 2N2222 before clipping. The positive and negative peaks clip at almost the exact same voltage level due to superior complementary matching.

2. High-Frequency Slew Limiting (LM741 Interaction)
If you turn your function generator up from 
1 kHz to 
10 kHz−20 kHz, the 741 op-amp's limited slew rate (
0.5 V/μs) becomes visible on the scope trace regardless of transistor choice:

The rounded sine wave turns into a triangular waveform because the 741 cannot change its output voltage fast enough to keep up with the input signal.

3. FFT Mode (Spectrum Analyzer View)
If your digital oscilloscope has an FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) button, this is where the differences become most dramatic. FFT breaks down the output signal into its individual harmonic frequencies:

    2N2222 / 2N2907 FFT (High Distortion)            BD139 / BD140 FFT (Clean Output)
